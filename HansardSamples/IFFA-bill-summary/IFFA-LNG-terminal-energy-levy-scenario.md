# Hypothetical Scenario: Offshore LNG Import Terminal Funded via Electricity Consumption Levy

## Revised Funding Model

Rather than a property-based levy collected via councils, this scenario envisages an **electricity consumption levy** (per MWh) collected via energy retailers and charged to all NZ electricity consumers. The justification: the LNG facility and associated natural gas infrastructure is required to maintain consistent, reliable electricity generation when NZ hydro dams cannot sustain generation due to climate change impacts (dry years, changed precipitation patterns).

---

## 1. Updated Levy Figures

### NZ Electricity Consumption Context

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Total NZ electricity consumption | ~42 TWh/year (42,000 GWh) |
| Number of electricity connections | ~2.1 million (residential + commercial + industrial) |
| Average household consumption | ~7,500 kWh/year (7.5 MWh) |
| Average residential electricity price | ~32 cents/kWh |
| Large industrial consumers (e.g. NZ Aluminium Smelter) | Up to 5,000 GWh/year each |

### Project Cost and Levy Calculation

Assuming a **$3 billion NZD** LNG import terminal (mid-range estimate), financed over **30 years** at approximately 6% interest:

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Total project cost | $3 billion |
| Annual SPV debt service (principal + interest) | ~$220 million/year |
| Annual levy per MWh (across 42 TWh) | **~$5.25/MWh** |
| Annual cost to average household (7.5 MWh) | **~$39/year** |
| Annual cost to small business (50 MWh) | **~$263/year** |
| Annual cost to medium manufacturer (5,000 MWh) | **~$26,250/year** |
| Annual cost to large industrial user (500 GWh) | **~$2.625 million/year** |
| Annual cost to NZ Aluminium Smelter-scale user (5 TWh) | **~$26.25 million/year** |

### Comparison with Property-Based Levy

| Model | Per-household annual cost | Pool size | Collection mechanism |
|-------|--------------------------|-----------|---------------------|
| Property levy (50,000 properties) | $700-$2,700/year | Local | Council rates |
| Property levy (100,000 properties) | $350-$1,350/year | Regional | Council rates |
| **Electricity levy (all NZ consumers)** | **~$39/year** | **National** | **Energy retailer bills** |

The electricity consumption model spreads the cost much more thinly across a vastly larger pool. This makes the per-household burden appear modest (~$3.25/month), but:

1. **Total extraction is the same** -- $220M/year leaves NZ electricity consumers regardless
2. **Industrial consumers bear a massive share** proportional to consumption, creating competitiveness concerns
3. The apparent affordability per household obscures the systemic cost -- every NZ business, school, hospital, and household pays
4. Large consumers like the Tiwai Point aluminium smelter (if still operating) would face a $26M/year additional cost -- potentially enough to trigger closure decisions

## 2. Amendments Required to the IFF Act

The current IFF Act (even as amended by the 2025 Bill) is fundamentally designed around **property-based levies collected by territorial authorities**. An electricity consumption levy would require substantial structural amendments across almost every part of the Act.

### 2.1 Purpose (Section 3) -- Already Partially Addressed

**Current position (as amended by the Bill):** Purpose is to fund infrastructure for "urban development" that (c) supports community needs and (d) appropriately allocates costs.

**Required change:** The purpose would need to be broadened further to encompass **national energy security infrastructure** -- not just urban development. Something like:

> *"...the provision of infrastructure for urban development **or nationally significant infrastructure that supports the security, reliability, or resilience of electricity supply**"*

**Assessment:** The Bill's repeal of purpose clauses (a) and (b) already loosens the purpose significantly. But "urban development" is still a core framing constraint. Energy infrastructure serving a national security-of-supply function is a different category entirely. This amendment would be a **fundamental change to the Act's scope**, taking it from a housing/urban tool to a general infrastructure financing mechanism.

### 2.2 Eligible Infrastructure (Section 8) -- Major Amendment Required

**Current position:** Eligible infrastructure is limited to:
- (a) water services infrastructure
- (b) transport infrastructure
- (c) community infrastructure or facilities
- (d) environmental resilience infrastructure

**Required change:** A new category would be needed:

> *"(e) energy infrastructure, meaning infrastructure for the generation, storage, transmission, or distribution of electricity, or for the supply of fuel used in electricity generation"*

Note how broadly this must be drafted to capture an LNG terminal: it's not electricity infrastructure per se, but fuel supply infrastructure *for* electricity generation. The drafting would need to cover:
- The LNG receiving terminal (FSRU or onshore)
- LNG storage tanks
- Regasification plant
- Gas pipeline connections to gas-fired power stations
- Potentially the gas-fired power stations themselves
- Port and marine infrastructure for LNG carrier vessels

**Assessment:** This is a significant expansion. The current categories all relate to things councils and water organisations typically provide. Energy infrastructure is regulated under the Electricity Industry Act 2010 and the Gas Act 1992 -- entirely different regulatory regimes. Importing energy infrastructure into the IFF Act creates jurisdictional overlap and potential conflict with the Electricity Authority's and Gas Industry Company's existing roles.

### 2.3 Responsible Levy Authority -- New Entity Type Required

**Current position (as amended by the Bill):** The RLA must be either:
- A territorial authority (council), or
- A water organisation (under the Local Government (Water Services) Act 2025)

**Required change:** Energy retailers (or a centralised entity like the Electricity Authority or Transpower) would need to be added as a new category of RLA. Options:

| Option | Entity | Pros | Cons |
|--------|--------|------|------|
| A | Each electricity retailer as RLA | Already bills consumers; competitive market | ~30+ retailers; coordination nightmare; some may refuse |
| B | Electricity Authority as RLA | Single entity; regulates market | Not a billing entity; would need new powers |
| C | Transpower (grid operator) as RLA | Already charges transmission levies | Only deals with generators/distributors, not consumers |
| D | New Crown entity as RLA | Purpose-built | Adds bureaucracy; new legislation likely needed |
| E | Lines companies as RLA | ~29 distributors; already charge fixed lines charges | Not retailers; consumers don't choose them |

**Assessment:** None of these fit cleanly. The Act's RLA concept is built around territorial authorities with rating databases and the power to collect rates. Energy retailers have billing relationships with consumers but no statutory rating power. This amendment would require either:
- Grafting quasi-rating powers onto energy retailers, or
- Creating a new centralised collection mechanism outside the RLA framework

Either approach is a substantial legislative change, likely requiring consequential amendments to the Electricity Industry Act 2010.

### 2.4 Levy Area -- Fundamental Reconceptualisation

**Current position:** *"levy area means the geographic area or areas of land in which a levy is authorised by a levy order"* (s7(1))

The entire Act is built on the concept that a levy applies to a **geographic area of land**. Every provision -- from levy proposals, to endorsements, to collection, to recovery -- references properties, rating units, and land.

**Required change:** For a consumption-based electricity levy, the "levy area" would need to become either:
- **All of New Zealand** (national levy), or
- A concept decoupled from land entirely -- perhaps an **"electricity supply area"** or **"levy consumer base"**

This is not a simple definitional change. It cascades through the entire Act:

| Current concept | Required replacement |
|----------------|---------------------|
| Levy area (geographic) | Electricity supply network (national) |
| Rating unit / property | Electricity connection point (ICP) |
| Ratepayer / property owner | Electricity consumer (account holder) |
| Levy per property | Levy per MWh consumed |
| Rating information database | Electricity registry / retailer billing systems |
| Council rates bill | Electricity bill line item |

**Assessment:** This is arguably the most fundamental change required. The Act's entire architecture assumes a land-based levy. Converting it to a consumption-based levy on a utility service is essentially building a different Act within the shell of the existing one.

### 2.5 Levypayer and Liability (Section 37) -- Complete Replacement

**Current position:** *"The ratepayer for a rating unit is liable to pay all levy that is due on the unit"* (s37(1))

**Required change:** New provisions would be needed to establish that:
- Every electricity consumer is a levypayer
- Liability accrues based on MWh consumed, not property ownership
- The retailer is the collection agent (analogous to PAYE for income tax)
- The levy appears as a separate line item on electricity bills

This raises significant questions:
- **Renters vs owners**: Under the property model, the property owner pays. Under the electricity model, whoever holds the electricity account pays -- typically the occupier. This shifts the levy burden to renters, who are generally lower-income.
- **Off-grid consumers**: Households and businesses with solar/battery systems consuming less grid electricity would pay less levy. Is this equitable if they still benefit from energy security?
- **Embedded networks**: Apartment buildings, shopping centres, and industrial parks with single grid connections would need allocation rules.

### 2.6 Levy Collection Mechanism (Part 3) -- Wholesale Replacement

**Current position:** Part 3 provides for levy collection through the rating system -- assessment notices, due dates, penalties, recovery via rating sale provisions of the Local Government (Rating) Act 2002.

**Required change:** An entirely new collection framework piggybacking on electricity billing:
- Retailers calculate levy based on metered consumption
- Levy is added to monthly/bimonthly electricity bills
- Retailers remit collected levies to the SPV (or a clearinghouse)
- Non-payment provisions would need to align with the Electricity Industry Act (disconnection, not rating sale)
- The Electricity Authority or Commerce Commission would likely need oversight of the levy pass-through

**Assessment:** This creates a fundamentally different enforcement regime. Currently, an unpaid IFF levy can lead to a rating sale (forced sale of land). For an electricity consumption levy, the enforcement mechanism would be **electricity disconnection** -- arguably an even more coercive power, given electricity is an essential service. The Electricity (Disconnection and Low Fixed Charge Tariff Option for Domestic Consumers) Regulations 2004 currently restrict disconnection rights. These would need to be reconciled with levy enforcement.

### 2.7 Infrastructure Endorsement (Section 20) -- New Endorsing Authority

**Current position:** The "responsible infrastructure authority" endorses the proposal. This is the entity in which the infrastructure will be vested -- typically a council or utility.

**Required change:** For an LNG terminal, the endorsing authority might be:
- **Transpower** (if the justification is grid reliability)
- **The Electricity Authority** (if framed as market security)
- **The Minister of Energy** (political endorsement)

Under the 2025 Bill, endorsement is already mandatory ("must endorse") if statutory requirements are met. Adding energy infrastructure and a new endorsing authority would extend this mandatory endorsement to energy regulators -- removing their discretion to oppose the project on energy policy grounds.

### 2.8 Protected Maori Land and Treaty Implications -- New Complexity

**Current position:** The Act and Bill deal with Maori land in the context of property-based levies -- land within a geographic levy area.

**Required change:** A national electricity consumption levy wouldn't target specific Maori land, but it would impose costs on all Maori households and businesses. Treaty of Waitangi implications arise differently:
- Electricity is an essential service; a compulsory levy on consumption has equity implications
- Maori households have, on average, lower incomes and higher energy poverty rates
- The levy funds fossil fuel infrastructure, which may conflict with Maori environmental and climate interests (kaitiakitanga)

The Act's existing Maori land protections (consent requirements, exemptions from recovery action) wouldn't apply because the levy isn't land-based. **New equity provisions** specifically addressing energy poverty and Treaty obligations would be needed.

### 2.9 Consultation -- Already Repealed (But Critically Needed Here)

**Current position (as amended by Bill):** Section 28 consultation is repealed entirely.

**Assessment:** For a property levy in a subdivision, removing ministerial consultation is questionable. For a **national electricity consumption levy funding fossil fuel infrastructure**, the absence of consultation is extraordinary. The Ministers who should be consulted include:
- **Minister of Energy** -- energy policy, market design, generation mix
- **Minister for Climate Change** -- emissions implications of new gas infrastructure
- **Minister of Finance** -- fiscal and economic impacts
- **Minister for the Environment** -- environmental effects
- **Minister of Commerce** -- competition effects on electricity market
- **Minister for Maori Development** -- Treaty and equity implications

Under the 2025 Bill, none of this consultation is required. The housing minister acts alone.

### 2.10 Accelerated Recovery -- Adaptation Required

**Current position (Bill):** The accelerated recovery mechanism targets "undeveloped land" -- if a developer fails to develop, the SPV can force sale of the land.

**Required change:** For an electricity consumption levy, there is no "land to sell" if the project fails. The accelerated recovery mechanism would need to be reconceived:
- **If the LNG terminal fails**: The SPV's recourse is against the terminal developer/landowner (as currently designed). This part could work as-is for the terminal site itself.
- **If consumers don't pay**: The enforcement mechanism shifts from forced land sale to **electricity disconnection** or debt collection -- a different legal framework entirely.
- **Stranded asset risk**: If the terminal becomes stranded (e.g., NZ transitions to renewables faster than projected), there's no "development failure" trigger -- the terminal exists, it's just not economically viable. The levy could continue for 30 years funding a white elephant, with no accelerated recovery mechanism to end it early.

## 3. Summary: Scale of Legislative Change Required

| Area | Change Required | Complexity |
|------|----------------|-----------|
| Purpose (s3) | Broaden to energy security | Moderate |
| Eligible infrastructure (s8) | Add energy infrastructure category | Moderate |
| Responsible Levy Authority | Add energy retailers or new entity | **High** |
| Levy area concept | Replace geographic area with national consumer base | **Fundamental** |
| Levypayer / liability (s37) | Replace property owner with electricity consumer | **High** |
| Collection mechanism (Part 3) | Replace rating system with electricity billing | **Fundamental** |
| Endorsement (s20) | Add energy sector endorsing authority | Moderate |
| Maori / Treaty provisions | New energy equity provisions | **High** |
| Consultation (s28) | Would need to be reinstated and expanded | Moderate (but politically unlikely) |
| Recovery mechanism | Adapt from land sale to disconnection/debt | **High** |
| Consequential amendments | Electricity Industry Act 2010, Gas Act 1992, Commerce Act 1986, Consumer Guarantees Act 1993 | **Extensive** |

**Overall assessment:** The amendments required go far beyond what the IFF Act was designed for. At least 4-5 of the changes are "fundamental" -- requiring reconceptualisation of core Act mechanisms rather than incremental amendment. In practice, using the IFF Act for this purpose would be legislating a substantially new financing regime inside the shell of a housing infrastructure Act.

A more honest legislative approach would be a standalone **Energy Security Infrastructure Financing Act** -- but that would attract far more parliamentary and public scrutiny than amending an existing Act that most people have never heard of. Using the IFF Act as a vehicle is, arguably, a strategy of **legislative concealment**: burying a nationally significant energy financing mechanism inside an obscure housing infrastructure statute.

## 4. The Democratic Deficit: Compounded

The 2025 Bill already removes consultation, affordability assessment, and council discretion for property-based levies. Extending these removals to a national electricity consumption levy would mean:

1. **Every NZ household and business pays** a compulsory levy on an essential service
2. **No affordability assessment** is required (s27A exemption applies if the developer "owns the land")
3. **No ministerial consultation** across energy, climate, finance, commerce, or Maori development portfolios
4. **No council discretion** to refuse endorsement
5. **No public consultation** of any kind
6. **Commercial confidentiality** protections limit what the public can learn about the deal structure
7. The **only democratic check** is a single Minister's personal decision, exercising reduced mandatory criteria

For a $3 billion fossil fuel infrastructure project funded by compulsory charges on every electricity consumer in New Zealand for 30 years, this represents a profound democratic deficit.

---

## 5. The Energy Security Argument: Legitimate Concern, Wrong Tool

The underlying concern -- NZ's vulnerability to dry-year hydro shortfalls, exacerbated by climate change -- is legitimate and widely recognised. The Electricity Authority's security-of-supply modelling consistently identifies this risk. But the IFF Act is the wrong tool because:

1. **The Electricity Authority already has market mechanisms** for security of supply (capacity markets, ancillary services, stress testing)
2. **The Commerce Commission regulates** electricity pricing and transmission investment
3. **The Gas Act 1992** governs gas infrastructure investment
4. **Transpower's grid investment** process has existing consultation and regulatory oversight
5. **The Resource Management Act** (or its replacement) governs environmental consenting

Each of these existing frameworks has **more public participation, regulatory oversight, and democratic accountability** than the IFF Act -- especially as amended by the 2025 Bill. Using the IFF Act to circumvent these frameworks would be a deliberate choice to avoid scrutiny.

The question is not whether NZ needs energy security investment. It is whether that investment should be funded through a mechanism designed to build suburban roads and stormwater drains, stripped of democratic safeguards, and charged to every household's power bill without anyone being asked.
