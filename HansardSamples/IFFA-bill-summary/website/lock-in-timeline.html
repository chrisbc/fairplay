The 30-Year Lock-In

When infrastructure becomes a stranded asset — rendered uneconomic by cheaper alternatives or geopolitical disruption — the levy continues regardless. There is no off-switch in the Act.

Year-by-Year: How Stranding Happens

Yr 0–5
Apparent success. Terminal commissioned. Gas-fired generation runs during dry hydro periods. Levy collected smoothly. A dry winter in year 3 validates the "energy security" argument. Political consensus: "it's working."
Yr 5–8
Warning signs. Global LNG prices rise (carbon pricing, supply disruptions). Solar + battery storage costs fall 40%. First large-scale iron-air battery installations commissioned. Electricity Authority modelling shows LNG-fired generation is 30–50% more expensive per MWh than renewables. Terminal capacity factor drops from 60% to 30%. The levy continues at full rate.
Yr 8–10
Stranding undeniable. Iron-air batteries reach $25/kWh. NZ renewable share exceeds 95%. LNG terminal operates only 20–30 days/year at high marginal cost. Industrial consumers threaten closure. Political pressure mounts to "turn off" the levy. But there is no legal mechanism to do so.
Yr 10–30
The Zombie Levy. Terminal mothballed or operating sporadically. SPV continues collecting $220M/year. Levy services debt — no longer funds infrastructure operation. Bondholders receive payments on schedule. NZ electricity consumers are paying $5.25/MWh for nothing. Total extraction over zombie period: ~$4.4 billion.

Could the Levy Be Stopped? Legal Options

Option 1: SPV voluntarily reduces (s52)

The SPV may reduce the levy if excess collection occurs — but the SPV has no incentive to reduce. "Excess" means over-collection relative to costs; as long as bonds are outstanding, costs always absorb the levy. Not available.

Option 2: Receiver appointed (s136)

If the SPV fails, a court appoints a receiver — to continue the levy, not end it. The asset failing is different from the SPV failing. A receiver makes things worse, not better. Not applicable.

Option 3: Governor-General revokes

Orders in Council can in principle be revoked, but the Act provides no express power to do so. Revocation would breach SPV contractual rights, triggering judicial review and compensation claims. Legally fraught.

Option 4: Parliament legislates

Parliament is sovereign and could terminate the levy by Act. But this amounts to expropriation of bondholders' rights — likely requiring $2–3B in compensation and damaging future infrastructure financing markets. Possible but extremely costly.

Option 5: Negotiate with bondholders

Buy out remaining bond obligations. But bondholders hold a statutory levy-backed asset — they have no incentive to discount. Any buyout would be at or near face value (~$2.3B remaining at year 10). Possible but expensive.

Option 6: Do nothing

The levy runs for 30 years as designed. Consumers pay $4.4B over 20 years for a mothballed facility. No legal action required. This is the default outcome.
