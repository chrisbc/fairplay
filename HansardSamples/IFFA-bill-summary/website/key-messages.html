Key Messages

Tailored summaries of the Amendment Bill's impact — for homeowners, renters, Māori communities, voters, energy advocates, and anyone trying to explain this legislation to a friend.

For homeowners
"You could be levied for 30 years for infrastructure you were never asked about, assessed for affordability, or given any say in — and there's no way out."
For renters
"Your landlord pays the levy. Your rent goes up. You had no voice in the decision and no protection against unaffordable charges being passed directly to you."
For Māori communities
"The bigger the project, the less your consent matters. The 5,000-property threshold removes consent rights for exactly the developments with the greatest impact on whenua Māori."
For voters
"One Minister, exercising fewer checks than ever, can commit your community to 30 years of compulsory charges — for infrastructure that could become a stranded asset within a decade."
For energy and climate advocates
"A mechanism designed for suburban stormwater could fund a 30-year fossil fuel lock-in via your electricity bill — with no mechanism to stop it when renewables make it redundant."
The bottom line
"The winners designed the rules. The losers pay the bills. Nobody is accountable if it goes wrong — because the Act has no accountability mechanism for bad infrastructure bets."

The Core Tension — One Sentence

"The Bill transfers decision-making power from democratic institutions to private capital, while transferring financial risk from private capital to the public."

For Different Contexts

In a letter to your MP

The Infrastructure Funding and Financing Amendment Bill 2025 removes six key public protections from the 2020 Act: mandatory ministerial consultation across five portfolios, affordability assessment, council endorsement discretion, Māori land consent for large projects, priority ranking of rates in recovery, and full public reporting. Collectively, these removals mean that a single Minister can impose a 30-year compulsory levy on communities with no independent scrutiny, no public participation, and no exit mechanism — even if the funded infrastructure becomes a stranded asset. I am writing to ask you to [support amendments / vote against the Bill].

Explaining it to a friend in two minutes

Imagine a developer wants to build infrastructure in your area. Under the new law, they propose a levy on all nearby properties — and one Minister approves it, with no requirement to consult you, check if it's affordable, or ask your council. Your council has to agree. The levy runs for 30 years. Even if the infrastructure is never finished, or becomes useless in year 10, you keep paying — because there's literally no legal mechanism to stop it. The developer got paid upfront. The investors holding the bonds get paid for 30 years regardless. And nobody is responsible if it goes wrong.

The energy security angle

The same mechanism designed to fund suburban water pipes could — with modest amendments or through a new "Energy Security Infrastructure" Act using the same template — fund fossil fuel infrastructure via every household's power bill, for 30 years, with no affordability assessment and no democratic check. By the time iron-air batteries and renewables have made that infrastructure redundant, you'd still be paying — and there would be no way to stop it short of a multi-billion dollar parliamentary intervention to buy out the bondholders.
