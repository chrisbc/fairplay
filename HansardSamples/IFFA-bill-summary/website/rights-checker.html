What Can You Do?

A citizen facing a levy under the amended Act would reasonably expect certain rights. The Amendment Bill 2025 removes almost all of them.

❌
Participate in consultation Section 28 ministerial consultation has been repealed entirely. There is no public participation process before a levy order is issued. Citizens have no formal channel to object.
❌
Require an affordability assessment Exempt if the developer owns the land, or obtains "written support" from current landowners — who are not the future levypayers who will bear the cost for 30 years.
❌
Rely on your council to block it Councils must endorse if statutory requirements are met. The only permitted ground for refusal is that rate collection itself would be materially compromised. Community concerns do not qualify.
❌
Stop paying if the asset becomes worthless The levy runs for the full 30-year period. No early termination mechanism exists under the Act. The levy continues even if infrastructure is idle, uneconomic, or demolished.
❌
Get a refund if too much is collected Excess levy is only assessed at the end of the levy period — not at any point during the 30 years of compulsory payment. Overpayment is reconciled in year 30, not year 5.
❌
Challenge the SPV's "failure" declaration The SPV self-certifies whether a development has "failed." A High Court Registrar (not a judge) can then order forced sale of land. The sale is explicitly valid even if irregular — and purchasers have no obligation to inquire whether the sale was properly conducted.
❌
See the full recommender's report OIA commercial confidentiality redactions are now permitted in published reports. The financial assumptions, risk assessments, and deal structure underlying a levy order may never be fully disclosed to the public.
⚠️
Vote out the Minister Yes — but the levy order is already made as an Order in Council. A new government faces the same legal constraint: no exit mechanism, and bondholder buyout costs potentially $2–3 billion. Electoral accountability cannot undo a 30-year statutory obligation.

The 30-Year Checklist

Situation Can a citizen… Answer
A levy is proposed for their area …participate in consultation? ❌ Consultation repealed
The levy appears unaffordable …require an affordability assessment? ❌ Exempt if developer owns land
The council opposes the levy …rely on council to block it? ❌ Council must endorse
Protected Māori land is included …require Māori consent for large projects? ❌ Removed at 5,000+ properties
The infrastructure becomes stranded …stop paying the levy? ❌ No early termination mechanism
The levy is excessive over time …get a refund mid-period? ❌ Only assessed at end of 30 years
The SPV fails …end the levy through the SPV? ❌ A receiver continues it
The Minister makes a bad decision …trigger an independent review? ❌ No review mechanism in the Act
They cannot pay …avoid forced sale of their land? ❌ After 4 months non-payment: SPV can force sale

What Citizens Can Do

Before the levy order
  • → Lobby Members of Parliament to oppose or amend the Bill
  • → Submit to the Select Committee during the Bill's passage
  • → Engage media and public debate to raise awareness
  • → Request council resolution opposing any endorsement
After the levy order
  • → Apply for judicial review of the levy order (limited grounds)
  • → Use OIA requests to obtain un-redacted information
  • → Advocate for new legislation to add protections
  • → Vote for representatives who commit to reform
The honest assessment

Once a levy order is issued, the practical options for individual citizens are extremely limited. The Act is designed to make the levy order final, binding, and resistant to challenge. Prevention — opposing the Bill before it passes — is vastly easier than remedy after the fact.

Analysis based on the Infrastructure Funding and Financing Act 2020 and Amendment Bill 2025
