Democratic Safeguards

The Act 2020 included six layers of public protection — ministerial consultation, affordability assessment, council discretion, Māori consent, rates priority, and full public reporting. The 2025 Bill removes or significantly weakens every one.

Why Each Safeguard Mattered

Multi-portfolio consultation

Five different Ministers brought expertise their own portfolio: commerce competition effects, consumer credit implications, local government funding pressures, rating law constraints, and fiscal risk to the Crown. One Minister lacks all of this.

Affordability assessment

Required analysis of whether levypayers could sustain payments over 30 years and what the long-term burden would be. Without it, a levy can be structured to look affordable per year while being crushing in aggregate or during economic downturns.

Council discretion

Councils are democratically elected bodies that understand their communities. Their ability to refuse endorsement was a critical local veto — one that represented community sovereignty over long-term financial commitments on their land.

Māori consent

Protected Māori land has special cultural and legal status under Te Tiriti o Waitangi. The consent requirement recognised that including such land in a compulsory levy area without agreement is an exercise of state power that requires Treaty-consistent process.

Rates priority

Councils collect rates to fund essential services — roads, water, parks, libraries. Rating priority in recovery sales ensured that if a property was sold to recover debts, the community's essential services funding came first.

Full public reporting

The recommender's report was the only independent public scrutiny of a levy proposal. Redacting it for commercial confidentiality means the public cannot assess whether the Minister was given accurate information, or what risks were identified and ignored.
