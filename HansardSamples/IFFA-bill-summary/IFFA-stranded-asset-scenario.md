# Stranded Asset Scenario: LNG Terminal Becomes Uneconomic Within 10 Years

## Premise

An offshore LNG import terminal has been built and funded under an IFF-style energy security levy mechanism (either via an amended IFF Act or a new Energy Security Infrastructure Financing Act). The key parameters:

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Total project cost | $3 billion NZD |
| Levy period | 30 years |
| Annual debt service | ~$220 million/year |
| Levy mechanism | ~$5.25/MWh on all NZ electricity consumption |
| Annual cost to average household | ~$39/year |
| Years of operation before stranding | ~10 years |
| Remaining debt at year 10 | ~$2.3 billion (principal + remaining interest) |

The terminal becomes a stranded asset within approximately 10 years due to one or both of the following:

### Trigger A: Gas Market Insecurity / Price Escalation

- Global LNG prices spike due to geopolitical disruption (e.g., further conflict in gas-producing regions, export restrictions, carbon border adjustments)
- NZ's LNG import price rises from an assumed ~$10-12/GJ to $20-30/GJ
- Gas-fired electricity generation becomes more expensive than alternatives
- The terminal operates at a fraction of capacity or intermittently
- The "energy security" rationale inverts: dependence on volatile imported LNG *increases* NZ's energy insecurity

### Trigger B: Alternative Technologies Mature

- **Iron-air grid-scale batteries** (e.g., Form Energy technology) reach commercial deployment at $20-30/kWh, providing 100+ hours of storage -- enough to bridge multi-day low-wind, low-hydro periods
- Combined with continued build-out of **wind** (onshore and offshore) and **solar** generation, NZ achieves sufficient dispatchable renewable capacity to cover dry-year hydro shortfalls
- The cost of renewable-plus-storage falls below the cost of LNG-fired generation (this crossover is projected by multiple analysts within the 2030s)
- The terminal's core justification -- dry-year energy security -- is met more cheaply, cleanly, and reliably by domestic renewable resources

## 1. The Critical Legal Gap: No "Off Switch"

### What the Act provides

The IFF Act contains provisions for:

- **Excess levy at end of levy period** (s91): If more levy has been collected than needed, the excess is credited back to levypayers. But this only applies at the *end* of the levy period -- i.e., after year 30.
- **Reduction of maximum levy revenue** (s52): The SPV *may* reduce the levy period or annual amount if excess levy exceeds a threshold. This is discretionary and relates to *over-collection*, not stranded assets.
- **Receiver appointment** (s136): If the SPV itself fails, a court may appoint a receiver to *continue exercising the SPV's levy powers*. This is designed to keep the levy running, not stop it.
- **Uncollected levy at end of period** (s93): The SPV may assign uncollected levy to the RLA after the period ends.

### What the Act does NOT provide

There is **no provision** for:

1. **Early termination of a levy** because the infrastructure is no longer needed, economic, or functional
2. **Stranded asset assessment** -- no trigger to re-evaluate whether the infrastructure still serves its purpose
3. **Consumer exit** -- no mechanism for levypayers to challenge continued payment for infrastructure that delivers no benefit
4. **Write-down of SPV debt** -- the SPV's bondholders have a statutory levy backing their investment; there is no mechanism to restructure this
5. **Ministerial intervention** to revoke or amend a levy order once made
6. **Public review** of whether the levy should continue

The levy order is an Order in Council (s29). Once made, it runs for its full period. The Governor-General made the order; there is no explicit power for the Governor-General (or anyone else) to revoke it early because circumstances have changed.

### The legal lock-in

This creates a situation where:

> **Every NZ electricity consumer continues paying ~$5.25/MWh for 20 more years to service the debt on infrastructure that is idle, uneconomic, or redundant -- and there is no legal mechanism to stop it.**

## 2. Year-by-Year Scenario

### Years 1-5: Apparent Success

- LNG terminal commissioned and operational
- Gas-fired peaking plants run during dry hydro periods
- The levy is collected smoothly via electricity bills
- Energy security argument appears validated during a dry winter in year 3
- Political consensus: "the levy is working"

### Years 5-8: Warning Signs

- Global LNG prices begin rising (carbon pricing, supply disruptions)
- Cost of solar + battery storage falls 40% (continuing historical trend)
- NZ's first large-scale iron-air battery installation (100 MWh, 100-hour duration) is commissioned
- Several new wind farms come online under the National Policy Statement for Renewable Electricity Generation
- Electricity Authority modelling shows LNG-fired generation is now 30-50% more expensive per MWh than renewable alternatives
- The terminal operates at declining capacity factor (from 60% to 30%)
- The levy continues at $5.25/MWh regardless

### Years 8-10: Stranding Becomes Undeniable

- Iron-air battery costs drop to $25/kWh; multiple grid-scale installations deployed
- NZ's renewable electricity share exceeds 95% (from ~82% today)
- The LNG terminal operates only 20-30 days per year, at high marginal cost
- Gas-fired generation is dispatched only as absolute last resort
- Industrial electricity consumers begin publicly objecting to the levy
- The NZ Aluminium Smelter (or equivalent large user paying ~$26M/year in levy) threatens closure, citing uncompetitive energy costs
- Political and media pressure mounts to "turn off" the levy
- **But there is no legal mechanism to do so**

### Years 10-15: The Zombie Levy

- The terminal is effectively mothballed or operates sporadically
- The SPV continues collecting $220M/year in levy revenue
- The levy revenue services debt -- it no longer funds infrastructure operation
- The SPV's bondholders continue receiving payments on schedule
- NZ electricity consumers are paying $5.25/MWh for nothing
- Total annual extraction: $220M from an economy that has moved on
- Cumulative cost to consumers over the "zombie" period (years 10-30): **~$4.4 billion** (in nominal terms)

### Years 15-30: Lock-in and Resentment

- The terminal may be physically decommissioned, but the levy continues
- A generation of NZ electricity consumers pays for a facility that was used for 8-10 years
- The levy becomes a symbol of poor infrastructure decision-making
- But the bondholders have a statutory right to the levy stream
- Political pressure eventually leads to legislative intervention -- but this requires an Act of Parliament, compensation for bondholders, and fiscal cost to the Crown

## 3. Winners and Losers

### Winners

| Party | How they win | Estimated gain |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| **SPV bondholders / investors** | Statutory levy-backed revenue stream continues for full 30 years regardless of asset utilisation. Effectively a government-guaranteed annuity. | $6.6 billion total levy revenue over 30 years (of which ~$4.4B is collected after the asset is stranded) |
| **International LNG consortium (original developer)** | Received $3B in IFF funding upfront. Built the terminal. Risk of stranding transferred entirely to NZ electricity consumers via the levy. The developer may have exited equity years ago. | $3B in construction revenue; minimal residual exposure |
| **Investment banks / arrangers** | Fees on structuring a $3B SPV bond issuance. No exposure to stranding risk. | Tens of millions in arrangement, legal, and advisory fees |
| **Renewable energy developers** | Ironically, they benefit from the levy making fossil fuel generation even less competitive. The levy is a de facto subsidy for their competitors that makes alternatives look even better. | Market share gain as LNG generation becomes uneconomic |
| **International battery manufacturers** | NZ becomes a buyer of grid-scale batteries to replace the function the LNG terminal was supposed to serve -- and NZ consumers are paying the LNG levy on top. | Sales of battery systems to NZ |

### Losers

| Party | How they lose | Estimated cost |
|-------|-------------|----------------|
| **NZ households (2.1 million)** | Pay ~$39/year for 30 years = ~$1,170 total per household. After year 10, they are paying for nothing. ~$780 per household is pure deadweight loss. | ~$780/household in stranded levy payments |
| **NZ businesses** | Pay levy proportional to consumption. Passed through to prices for goods and services. Reduces NZ competitiveness. | Varies; large consumers lose millions/year |
| **Large industrial users** | Pay millions/year in levy for a facility that no longer operates. May trigger closures or relocation. | $2.6M - $26M/year for 20 unnecessary years |
| **Low-income households** | Electricity is a larger share of their expenditure. The levy is regressive -- everyone pays the same $/MWh regardless of ability. No affordability assessment was conducted (s27A exemption). | Disproportionate burden relative to income |
| **Maori communities** | Higher energy poverty rates; disproportionate levy burden. Infrastructure may have been sited near Maori communities. No consultation (s28 repealed). | Economic and environmental burden |
| **The Crown / NZ taxpayer** | If political pressure eventually forces early termination, the Crown will need to compensate bondholders for the remaining ~$2.3B in expected levy revenue. This becomes a fiscal liability. | Potentially $2-3B in bondholder compensation |
| **Future electricity consumers** | Inherit a levy obligation for infrastructure they never chose and never benefited from. A 20-year-old in year 1 pays until age 50 for a facility that was useful for 10 years. | 20 years of unnecessary payment |
| **NZ's climate commitments** | The levy subsidises fossil fuel infrastructure, distorting energy investment decisions and slowing the renewable transition during the critical 2030s decade. | Delayed decarbonisation |

### The Asymmetry

The core asymmetry is temporal and informational:

- **Bondholders** locked in a 30-year guaranteed revenue stream at the outset, when the project looked viable
- **Consumers** are locked in for 30 years with no exit, no review, and no ability to challenge the levy's continuation
- **The developer** received funding upfront and has no obligation if the project becomes uneconomic
- **The Minister** approved the levy based on a single assessment (with reduced criteria under the Bill), with no requirement to revisit the decision
- **No one** is responsible for the stranded asset outcome -- the Act has no accountability mechanism for bad infrastructure bets

## 4. Could the Levy Be Stopped? Legal Options

### Option 1: SPV Voluntarily Reduces Levy (s52)

The SPV *may* reduce the levy period or annual levy amount if excess levy exceeds a threshold. But:
- The SPV has no incentive to reduce -- it needs the levy to service its debt
- "Excess levy" means over-collection relative to costs. If the SPV's costs include debt service (which they do), the levy is never "excess" as long as bonds are outstanding
- **Conclusion: Not available**

### Option 2: Receiver Appointed (s136)

A court can appoint a receiver if the SPV fails. But:
- The SPV hasn't failed -- it's collecting levy as designed. The *asset* has failed, not the SPV.
- A receiver would be appointed to *continue* the levy, not end it
- **Conclusion: Makes things worse, not better**

### Option 3: Governor-General Revokes the Levy Order

The levy order is an Order in Council (s29). In principle, Orders in Council can be revoked. But:
- The Act provides no express power to revoke a levy order
- Revoking the order would breach the SPV's contractual rights with bondholders
- Bondholders would have grounds for judicial review and/or contractual damages
- **Conclusion: Legally fraught; would trigger litigation and compensation claims**

### Option 4: Parliament Legislates to Terminate

Parliament is sovereign and could pass an Act terminating the levy. But:
- This would amount to **expropriation** of the bondholders' contractual rights
- Under the NZ Bill of Rights Act 1990, s21 (unreasonable search and seizure) and the common law of property rights, compensation would likely be required
- The fiscal cost could be $2-3 billion in bondholder buyout
- Sets a precedent that undermines future infrastructure financing (who would invest in SPV bonds if Parliament might cancel them?)
- **Conclusion: Possible but extremely costly and precedent-damaging**

### Option 5: Negotiate with Bondholders

The Crown (or a Crown entity) could negotiate to buy out the remaining bond obligations at a discount. But:
- Bondholders hold a statutory levy-backed asset -- one of the safest infrastructure investments possible
- They have no incentive to accept a discount; the levy will keep paying
- Any buyout would be at or near face value (~$2.3B remaining at year 10)
- **Conclusion: Possible but expensive; bondholders hold all the cards**

### Option 6: Do Nothing

- The levy runs for 30 years as designed
- Consumers pay $4.4B over the remaining 20 years for a mothballed facility
- No legal action required
- **Conclusion: This is the default outcome -- and the most likely one**

## 5. The Perverse Incentives

### 5.1 Incentive to Keep the Terminal Running (Even Uneconomically)

The SPV and its investors may lobby to keep the terminal operating even when alternatives are cheaper, because:
- Operating justifies the levy politically
- A mothballed terminal with a running levy is politically toxic
- Continued operation, even at a loss, maintains the narrative that the levy serves a purpose
- The SPV can use levy revenue to subsidise uneconomic LNG purchases, keeping the terminal nominally active

This creates a **subsidy treadmill**: the levy subsidises uneconomic gas generation, which displaces cheaper renewables, which delays the transition, which extends the period during which the terminal appears "needed."

### 5.2 Incentive Against Alternative Investment

While the levy is running:
- Every MWh of renewable generation that displaces gas generation *still pays the levy*
- Investing in batteries or renewables doesn't reduce the levy
- The levy makes NZ electricity more expensive, potentially slowing electrification of transport and industry
- Alternative energy security investments (batteries, demand response, pumped hydro) must compete against "free" (levy-funded) gas backup

The levy **distorts the entire energy market** by providing gas-fired generation with a cost advantage funded by all other generation and consumption.

### 5.3 Incentive to Build the Wrong Infrastructure

If this model "works" for LNG, it creates a template:
- Any large energy project could seek IFF-style levy funding
- Proponents have incentive to overstate energy security risks to justify the levy
- The absence of affordability assessment, consultation, and council discretion means there are few checks on overbuilding
- NZ could accumulate multiple levy-funded white elephants, each with 30-year lock-ins

## 6. What Would Have Been Different With Democratic Safeguards?

Consider what would happen if the original 2020 Act's safeguards had been retained and applied:

| Safeguard | How it would have helped |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Affordability assessment** (s27(5)(c)) | Would have examined whether a 30-year levy for volatile-cost gas infrastructure is sustainable for consumers |
| **Long-term interest of levypayers** (s27(5)(b)) | Would have required analysis of stranding risk and technology change over 30 years |
| **Ministerial consultation** (s28) | Commerce Minister would have assessed competition effects on the electricity market; Finance Minister would have flagged fiscal risk of bondholder lock-in |
| **Council discretion** (s20-21) | Local authorities near the terminal could have refused endorsement based on community opposition or environmental concerns |
| **Maori consent** (s24) | Iwi could have assessed cultural, environmental, and economic impacts before consenting |
| **Recommender's analysis** (s25-26) | Full analysis would have included technology risk, market risk, and stranding scenarios |
| **Published report** (s30) | Public would have been able to scrutinise assumptions about gas prices, demand, and alternatives |

None of these safeguards would necessarily have prevented the project. But they would have forced consideration of the risks that materialised. A rigorous affordability assessment in particular would have asked: *what happens if this $3 billion asset becomes worthless in 10 years, and consumers are locked into the levy for 20 more?*

The 2025 Bill removed or weakened every one of these safeguards.

## 7. The Iron-Air Battery Counterfactual

Consider the alternative: instead of a $3B LNG terminal funded by compulsory levy, NZ had invested the same amount in grid-scale iron-air batteries and associated renewable generation.

| Parameter | LNG Terminal (Levy-Funded) | Iron-Air Batteries + Renewables |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Capital cost | $3B (SPV-financed, levy-repaid) | $3B (could also be SPV-financed, or market-funded) |
| Fuel cost | $10-30/GJ for imported LNG (volatile, geopolitically exposed) | $0 (wind, solar, hydro) |
| Carbon emissions | ~0.5 Mt CO2/year when operating | Zero |
| Import dependency | 100% -- NZ imports all LNG | Zero -- domestic resources |
| Technology risk | High -- fossil fuel assets face stranding as renewables improve | Low -- batteries complement renewables; no fuel cost exposure |
| Duration of usefulness | 8-10 years (before stranding) | 20-30+ years (batteries degrade but are recyclable; renewables are long-lived) |
| Stranding risk | Very high -- gas price and technology disruption | Low -- electricity demand is growing; storage always has value |
| Energy security | Dependent on global LNG supply chains | Sovereign -- NZ controls its own renewable resources |
| 30-year total cost to consumers | ~$6.6B in levy + gas purchase costs | ~$3B in capital repayment; minimal operating costs |
| Residual value at year 30 | Decommissioning liability | Functioning (if degraded) storage assets + operating renewables |

The bitter irony: the levy mechanism designed to fund "energy security" infrastructure locks NZ into the *least secure* energy pathway -- imported fossil fuel with volatile pricing and stranding risk -- while the levy itself makes the *most secure* pathway (domestic renewables + storage) relatively more expensive for consumers.

## 8. Conclusion: The Accountability Vacuum

The stranded LNG terminal scenario exposes a fundamental design flaw in the IFF Act as a vehicle for nationally significant infrastructure:

**There is no accountability for bad infrastructure decisions.**

- The **developer** received funding and left
- The **SPV** is a special-purpose shell that exists only to collect the levy and service debt
- The **bondholders** have a statutory right to payment regardless of asset performance
- The **Minister** approved the levy under reduced criteria, with no requirement to revisit
- The **recommender** published an (possibly redacted) report and moved on
- The **monitor** watches the SPV operate, not whether the infrastructure makes sense

No person or institution is responsible for asking, at year 10: *should we still be doing this?*

The Act creates a self-sustaining financial mechanism -- the levy feeds the SPV, which feeds the bondholders -- that operates independently of whether the underlying infrastructure serves any public purpose. It is, in effect, a **perpetual motion machine of public cost extraction**, locked in by statute and insulated from democratic correction.

The winners understood the game from the beginning: get the levy order made, issue the bonds, build the asset, and walk away. The levy does the rest for 30 years, regardless of what happens in the real world. The losers -- every NZ electricity consumer -- discover the game only when it's too late to change the rules.
