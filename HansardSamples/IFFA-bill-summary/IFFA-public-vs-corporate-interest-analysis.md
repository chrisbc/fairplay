## 1. The Balance of Interests: How the 2025 Bill Shifts Power

### 1.1 Purpose Clause Narrowing (Clause 4 of the Bill)

**Act 2020, Section 3(1)** stated the purpose was to provide infrastructure funding that:
- **(a)** supports the functioning of urban land markets; and
- **(b)** reduces the impact of local authority financing and funding constraints; and
- (c) supports community needs; and
- (d) appropriately allocates the costs of infrastructure.

**The Bill repeals (a) and (b).** The explanatory note frames this as "broadening" the purpose, but it also removes the two clauses that tied the Act's use to *public-interest* rationales: functioning land markets (a public good) and addressing council financing gaps (a democratic governance concern). What remains -- "supports community needs" and "appropriate cost allocation" -- are vaguer and more easily satisfied by private-interest projects.

**Trade-off**: The developer gains a wider playing field; citizens lose a purpose clause that anchored the Act in solving *public* funding problems.

### 1.2 Removal of Consultation Requirements (Clause 18)

**Act 2020, Section 28** required the Minister to consult with Ministers responsible for the Commerce Act, the Credit Contracts and Consumer Finance Act, the Local Government Act, the Local Government (Rating) Act, and the Public Finance Act before recommending a levy order.

**The Bill repeals Section 28 entirely.**

This is perhaps the single most significant democratic erosion. Section 28 ensured that levy orders -- which impose compulsory charges on property owners -- were scrutinised through the lenses of:
- **Competition law** (Commerce Act) -- are monopoly rents being created?
- **Consumer protection** (CCFA) -- are levypayers being treated fairly?
- **Local governance** (LGA) -- is this consistent with democratic local decision-making?
- **Rating fairness** (Rating Act) -- is the levy structured equitably?
- **Public finance** (PFA) -- is the Crown's fiscal position affected?

By repealing this, the Bill removes five independent ministerial checks. A developer-led levy proposal will face only the recommender and the responsible Minister -- with reduced criteria (see below).

**Trade-off**: Developers gain speed and certainty; citizens lose five layers of cross-portfolio scrutiny designed to catch problems the housing minister alone might miss.

### 1.3 Affordability Assessment Exemption (New Section 27A)

**Act 2020, Section 27(5)(b) and (c)** required the Minister to consider:
- (b) the long-term interests of levypayers
- (c) the affordability of the levy for levypayers and the sustainability of its payment by them over the levy period

**New Section 27A** provides that neither the recommender nor the Minister is required to consider these matters if:
- The land is **owned by the levy proposer** (i.e. the developer itself), OR
- All owners/purchasers have provided **written support** for the levy.

The Bill's explanatory note justifies this by claiming that in greenfield developments, "levypayers self-assess affordability and opt into the levy when deciding to purchase a property."

**Critical problems with this reasoning:**

1. **Information asymmetry**: Future home buyers may not understand the long-term levy burden when purchasing. A levy is not like a purchase price -- it is a recurring obligation that attaches to the land and may last decades.

2. **Coercive consent**: When a developer owns all the land and "consents" on behalf of future owners, the consent is self-dealing. The actual future levypayers (home buyers) never had a seat at the table.

3. **Written support under duress**: In a constrained housing market, buyers desperate for housing may sign support for levies they cannot actually afford long-term.

4. **Brownfield loophole**: While "targeted at greenfield developments," the Bill explicitly acknowledges the exemption also applies to brownfield (existing urban) developments where existing owners are pressured to support a levy.

**Trade-off**: Developers gain exemption from the most meaningful public-interest test in the Act; citizens who will live with the levy for decades lose their main protection against unaffordable charges.

### 1.4 Mandatory Endorsement (Clauses 12-13)

**Act 2020, Section 20**: A responsible infrastructure authority "*may* endorse" a levy proposal.

**Act 2020, Section 21**: Provided for discretionary levy endorsement by the proposed RLA.

**The Bill changes "may" to "must"** in Section 20(1), requiring endorsement if statutory requirements are met. New Section 21 replaces the old provision: a proposed RLA "*must* endorse" the levy unless it can demonstrate the levy will "compromise its ability to collect rates."

Further, **new Section 20(5)** removes the endorsement requirement entirely for developer-owned infrastructure not vested in a public authority.

**Trade-off**: Councils lose their discretion to refuse endorsement based on broader community concerns (traffic, amenity, environmental impact, community opposition). The only ground for refusal is the narrow technical question of whether rate collection is compromised. Developers gain near-automatic endorsement.

### 1.5 Protected Maori Land: The 5,000-Property Threshold (Clause 14)

**Act 2020, Section 24** required consent from owners of protected Maori land to include that land in a levy area -- in all cases.

**New Section 24(3)** removes this consent requirement where the proposed levy area encompasses **5,000 or more leviable properties** over the levy period.

The Bill's justification is to "distinguish between greenfield proposals...and citywide levy proposals to deliver infrastructure for the wider community." But the practical effect is that the larger the project, the less protection Maori landowners have -- precisely the scenario where Treaty of Waitangi interests are most at risk.

Notably, recovery action (rating sale/lease) is prohibited on protected Maori land, so this provision creates a curious dynamic: Maori land can be included in a levy area without consent, but the levy cannot be forcibly recovered from it. This may result in the cost burden being shifted to *other* levypayers in the area.

**Trade-off**: Large-scale developers can bypass Maori consent for projects affecting 5,000+ properties; Maori landowners lose their veto right in precisely the cases where the development impact is greatest.

### 1.6 Levy-vs-Rates Ranking (Amendments to Local Government (Rating) Act 2002)

**Act 2020, Section 84**: Rates ranked first in the proceeds of a rating sale. Levies were subordinate.

**The Bill**: Levies now rank **equally** alongside rates. If proceeds are insufficient, they are distributed **proportionally** between rates and levies.

This is a significant shift in financial risk. Councils (and therefore ratepayers) previously had priority recovery. Now the SPV (a private entity or Crown entity) shares equally. This means that in a failed development, the council may recover less of its rates, increasing the burden on the wider ratepayer base.

**Trade-off**: SPVs (often backed by corporate investors) gain improved recovery position; councils and general ratepayers bear more risk of shortfall.

### 1.7 Accelerated Recovery of IFF Funding (New Subpart 2A of Part 4)

The Bill introduces an entirely new accelerated recovery mechanism (sections 98A-98N):

1. The levy order can specify **factors that determine a "development failure"**
2. If those factors are met, the SPV issues a **written demand** for repayment of IFF funding plus financing costs of unpaid levies
3. If not paid within **20 working days**, the SPV may apply to a **Registrar of the High Court** (not a judge) to sell the undeveloped land
4. The formula for repayment is: `r = (a x b) / c` where `a` = IFF funding provided, `b` = undeveloped land area, `c` = total levy area

This is a remarkably powerful enforcement mechanism:
- **20 working days** is an extremely short window for a landowner to find potentially millions of dollars
- The application is to a **Registrar** (an administrative officer), not a High Court judge -- bypassing judicial scrutiny
- The SPV itself determines whether the "failure factors" are met
- The **$1,078 application fee** is trivial relative to the amounts involved

**Trade-off**: SPVs gain a near-automatic forced sale power with minimal judicial oversight; landowners (including failed developers, but also potentially individual lot owners within the development area) face rapid dispossession.

### 1.8 Publication and Transparency (Clause 19)

**New Section 30(2)** provides that the monitor is "not required to publish information in the recommender's report that could properly be withheld under the Official Information Act 1982."

While the OIA already permits withholding of commercially sensitive information, explicitly embedding this exemption in the publication requirement signals that less information about levy proposals will be publicly available. Given that consultation has been removed, transparency in the recommender's published report was one of the few remaining checks.

**Trade-off**: Developers gain commercial confidentiality protections; public scrutiny is reduced.

## 2. Scorecard: Who Benefits?

| Change | Developer/Corporate Benefit | Public/Citizen Cost |
|--------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Purpose clause narrowed | Wider applicability | Loss of public-interest anchoring |
| Consultation repealed | Faster approvals | Loss of 5 ministerial cross-checks |
| Affordability exemption (s27A) | No affordability scrutiny for own land | Future homeowners unprotected |
| Mandatory endorsement | Near-automatic council approval | Councils lose discretion |
| Maori land consent removed (5000+) | Large projects proceed without consent | Indigenous rights diminished |
| Levy = rates ranking | Better recovery position | Council/ratepayer recovery risk |
| Accelerated recovery | Rapid forced land sale | 20-day dispossession risk |
| OIA transparency carve-out | Commercial confidentiality | Reduced public information |

**Overall direction**: The Bill systematically removes barriers, checks, and democratic participation mechanisms that existed to protect public interests, replacing them with streamlined, developer-friendly processes where the main remaining constraint is the Minister's personal judgement -- exercising reduced mandatory considerations.

## 3. Hypothetical Scenario: Offshore LNG Import Terminal Under the IFF Act

### 3.1 The Scenario

Imagine a proposal for a large-scale **Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) import terminal** at a New Zealand port city -- perhaps Marsden Point (Northland) or New Plymouth. The project would include:

- A floating storage and regasification unit (FSRU) or onshore terminal
- Pipeline connections to the national gas grid
- Port upgrades, road access, and associated infrastructure
- Total cost: approximately **$2-4 billion NZD**
- Justified as addressing NZ's declining domestic gas production and energy security

The developer is a consortium of international energy companies and NZ-based infrastructure investors.

### 3.2 How the IFF Act (as amended) Could Be Used

**Step 1: SPV Formation**

The consortium forms an SPV -- a special purpose vehicle company. Under the Act, this SPV is the entity that borrows and spends. Because SPV financing is "off balance sheet" for councils and Crown entities, the true scale of public liability is obscured.

**Step 2: Defining "Eligible Infrastructure"**

The 2020 Act restricted eligible infrastructure to things supporting "urban development" -- housing, roads, water, stormwater. The Bill broadens the purpose by removing clauses (a) and (b), and the expanded scope now includes NZTA/KiwiRail transport infrastructure.

An LNG terminal proponent might argue that:
- Port and road upgrades are "transport infrastructure" supporting urban development
- Gas supply underpins industrial and residential development
- Associated worker housing and community facilities qualify as "urban development"

The broadened purpose clause and expanded entity scope (NZTA, KiwiRail, water organisations) make such creative framing more plausible.

**Step 3: Levy Proposal**

The SPV proposes a levy on properties in the surrounding district -- say, all properties within a 50km radius of the terminal. The levy area could easily encompass **well over 5,000 properties** (triggering the Maori land consent exemption) and include:
- Residential homeowners
- Commercial property owners
- Industrial sites
- Protected Maori land (without requiring consent)

**Step 4: Bypassing Safeguards**

Under the amended Act:

1. **No affordability assessment** (s27A): If the developer owns the terminal site and associated land, or if existing landowners can be pressured into providing "written support," the Minister need not consider whether the levy is affordable for householders.

2. **No consultation** (s28 repealed): The Minister acts alone without consulting the Commerce Minister (competition concerns about monopoly gas infrastructure), the Consumer Finance Minister (fairness of the charges), or the Public Finance Minister (Crown fiscal exposure).

3. **Mandatory endorsement**: The council *must* endorse the levy unless it can prove it would compromise rate collection -- a narrow technical test that says nothing about whether an LNG terminal is appropriate infrastructure.

4. **Maori consent bypassed**: With 5,000+ properties in the levy area, owners of protected Maori land -- potentially including significant coastal and harbour-adjacent land with cultural significance -- have no consent right.

5. **Reduced transparency**: The recommender's report can withhold commercially sensitive information under the OIA exemption, meaning the public may never see the full financial structure, risk allocation, or projected levy burden.

### 3.3 The Levy Burden

Under this scenario, the SPV borrows $2-4 billion and repays through levies on perhaps 50,000-100,000 properties over a 30-year period. This translates to:

- **Annual levy per property**: Approximately $700-$2,700/year (on top of existing rates)
- **Total per-property cost over 30 years**: $21,000-$81,000

These are rough estimates, but they illustrate the scale. For context, the median NZ household income is approximately $110,000. An annual levy of $1,000-$2,700 represents 1-2.5% of gross household income -- a significant burden, particularly for lower-income households.

Under the amended Act, **no one is required to assess whether this is affordable.**

### 3.4 Who Actually Benefits?

| Stakeholder | Benefit | Cost |
|------------|---------|------|
| **International LNG consortium** | Off-balance-sheet financing; levypayers bear the cost; accelerated recovery if it fails | Minimal -- risk is transferred |
| **SPV investors/bondholders** | Secure levy-backed revenue stream; equal ranking with rates; accelerated forced sale if project fails | Minimal -- government-guaranteed revenue |
| **NZ gas consumers** | Access to imported LNG (possibly at higher prices than domestic gas) | Pay both market gas prices AND the levy |
| **Homeowners in levy area** | Potentially improved energy security | $700-$2,700/year levy for 30 years, no affordability check |
| **Maori landowners** | None -- infrastructure may damage cultural sites, coastline | No consent required; land included without agreement |
| **Council/ratepayers** | Rates now share recovery equally with levy SPV | Reduced recovery in default; increased administrative burden |
| **The Minister** | Political credit for "infrastructure delivery" | Personal decision with reduced checks and no required consultation |

### 3.5 What Could Go Wrong

1. **Stranded asset risk**: Global LNG prices are volatile. NZ may transition to renewables faster than projected. The terminal could become a stranded asset -- but the levy continues. Homeowners keep paying for infrastructure that delivers no value.

2. **Development failure**: If the project fails, the SPV has **accelerated recovery powers** -- it can demand full repayment within 20 working days and apply to a Registrar (not a judge) to force sale of undeveloped land. This could trigger forced land sales in the surrounding area.

3. **Distributional injustice**: Low-income households, renters (whose landlords pass on the levy), and Maori communities bear a disproportionate burden for infrastructure that primarily benefits gas industry shareholders.

4. **Democratic deficit**: No public consultation. No cross-ministerial review. Council endorsement is mandatory. The only public disclosure is a recommender's report that can be redacted for commercial sensitivity. Citizens in the levy area may learn of the levy only when it appears on their rates bill.

5. **Environmental cost externalisation**: The IFF Act makes no provision for environmental impact assessment as part of the levy process. A fossil fuel infrastructure project funded by compulsory levies on homeowners inverts the "polluter pays" principle.

### 3.6 Could This Actually Happen?

The IFF Act's original design -- focused on housing-related infrastructure with council discretion, affordability assessment, and ministerial consultation -- made such a scenario implausible. The Amendment Bill's changes collectively lower the barriers enough that a creative proponent could construct a viable application:

- The broadened purpose and entity scope opens the door
- The removal of council discretion prevents local democratic opposition
- The affordability exemption silences the most obvious objection
- The consultation repeal removes cross-portfolio checks
- The Maori consent threshold removes indigenous opposition for large projects
- The accelerated recovery mechanism protects the SPV's investors
- The transparency carve-out shields the deal structure from public scrutiny

Whether the Minister would approve such a proposal is a political question. But the Bill's design philosophy -- trust the market, trust the developer, streamline the process -- leaves the Minister as the sole meaningful check, exercising reduced criteria, without required consultation.

## 4. Conclusion

The IFF Amendment Bill 2025 represents a systematic rebalancing of power from public/democratic interests toward private/corporate development interests. Every major amendment reduces public protection, scrutiny, or participation:

1. **Democratic checks removed**: Consultation repealed, council discretion eliminated, affordability assessment waived
2. **Indigenous rights diminished**: Maori consent bypassed for large developments
3. **Financial risk shifted**: From developers/SPVs to homeowners and ratepayers
4. **Enforcement asymmetry**: Developers gain accelerated forced sale; homeowners gain only the right to pay
5. **Transparency reduced**: OIA carve-out in publication requirements

The LNG terminal scenario -- while hypothetical -- illustrates how a sufficiently large and well-connected proponent could use these provisions to socialise the costs of major private infrastructure while privatising the benefits. The Bill's explanatory note frames everything as "removing barriers" and "improving usability." But the barriers being removed are the democratic safeguards that ensure infrastructure serves the public interest rather than just the developer's balance sheet.

The fundamental question the Bill raises is: **who should bear the risk and cost of infrastructure?** The 2020 Act answered: *those who benefit, with democratic oversight and affordability protections.* The 2025 Bill's answer increasingly becomes: *homeowners, automatically, with minimal oversight.*
