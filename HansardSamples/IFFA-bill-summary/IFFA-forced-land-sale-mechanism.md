# The Forced Land Sale Mechanism: A Detailed Analysis

## Source: Infrastructure Funding and Financing Amendment Bill 2025, New Subpart 2A of Part 4 (Sections 98A-98N)

---

## 1. What the Provisions Actually Say

The Bill inserts an entirely new **Subpart 2A** (sections 98A-98N) into the Act, titled *"Accelerated recovery of IFF funding when development has failed."* This creates a rapid, largely administrative process by which an SPV can force the sale of land without meaningful judicial oversight.

---

## 2. Whose Land Is Sold?

The target is specifically **"undeveloped land"** -- land within a levy area that has not yet been developed. The **"landowner"** is defined as *"the person that owns the land in relation to which the development has failed"* (s98A).

In practice this means the **developer itself** (or a subsequent purchaser of the raw land). This is *not* aimed at homeowners who have already built and occupied houses. It targets the entity holding undeveloped lots within a subdivision or project area.

Importantly, protected Maori land is explicitly excluded (s98B(a)).

---

## 3. What Triggers It?

The levy order itself must specify **"factors that must be used to determine when a land development has failed"** (s98C(1), referencing new s34(1)(l)). These factors are bespoke to each project -- the Bill doesn't define "failure" generically. Examples might include:

- Fewer than X% of lots developed within Y years
- Developer enters receivership/liquidation
- Building consents not obtained by a milestone date
- Infrastructure construction not commenced or completed by a deadline

The critical point: **the SPV itself decides** whether the factors are met. Section 98C(2) says *"If a responsible SPV is satisfied that 1 or more of the factors apply..."* -- there is no independent arbiter at this stage.

---

## 4. The Timeline (Shockingly Fast)

### Step 1: SPV Demand Notice (Day 0)

The SPV decides the development has failed and issues a written demand to the landowner for repayment of:

- **(a)** the IFF funding that relates to the undeveloped land, calculated by the formula:

  > `r = (a x b) / c`
  >
  > where:
  > - `r` = amount to be repaid
  > - `a` = amount of IFF funding the SPV provided to the landowner towards construction costs
  > - `b` = geographic area of undeveloped land owned by the relevant landowner
  > - `c` = total levy area

- **(b)** the **financing costs** of any unpaid levies on that land

The notice must warn that the SPV will apply to the High Court Registrar for a forced sale if payment is not made within 20 working days (s98C(3)). A copy must be sent to the monitor (s98C(5)).

### Step 2: Landowner Payment Window (Day 0 + 20 working days, ~4 calendar weeks)

If the landowner hasn't paid **in full**, the SPV may apply to a **Registrar of the High Court** (not a judge) for a forced sale. The application requires only:

- A certificate signed by the **SPV's own CEO** stating the amounts owed, the landowner's details, and the land description (s98E(3)(a))
- An affidavit of service proving the notice was delivered (s98E(3)(b))
- An application fee of **$1,078** (as inserted into the High Court Fees Regulations 2013 by clause 73 and Schedule 5 of the Bill)

### Step 3: Registrar's Notice (~Day 30+)

The Registrar notifies all persons believed to have an interest in the undeveloped land that it will be sold unless, within **10 working days**, payments are made to the High Court covering:

- The IFF funding repayment amount
- The financing costs of any unpaid levies
- Any **disbursements incurred related to the sale process** (s98F(2)(b))

### Step 4: Sale Proceeds (~Day 0 + ~6-8 calendar weeks)

If still unpaid after 10 working days, the **Registrar proceeds to sell** the land (s98F(4)).

**Total elapsed time from the SPV's unilateral decision to forced sale: approximately 6-8 weeks.**

---

## 5. How the Sale Is Conducted

### Public auction or tender (s98G)

- The sale must be by **public auction or public tender** (s98G(1))
- The Registrar may place a reserve, refuse tenders, or **bid for and buy the land themselves** (s98G(2)(c))
- The purchaser is the highest bidder/tenderer, provided the bid equals or exceeds the IFF funding repayment amount plus disbursements (s98G(3))

### Private treaty fallback (s98H)

- If the auction fails and the Registrar has placed a reserve, the Registrar may sell by **private treaty** with the SPV's consent, for whatever price the Registrar **"thinks reasonable"** (s98H(1))
- The Registrar may include covenants and conditions they consider "fair and equitable" (s98H(2))

### Transfer of title (s98I)

- The Registrar executes the memorandum of transfer **under the seal of the High Court** on behalf of the landowner (s98I(1)(a)) -- the landowner has no say
- The purchaser is entitled to obtain possession upon execution of the transfer (s98I(2))

### Validity protections for the buyer (s98J)

- **"A sale made under section 98G or 98H is valid for all purposes, even if there has been an irregularity in the conduct of the sale"** (s98J(1))
- **"No purchaser is obliged to inquire whether the sale was properly conducted"** (s98J(2))

---

## 6. Where the Money Goes

1. Sale proceeds are applied **as specified in the levy order** (s98K(1)) -- i.e., in accordance with terms set at the outset, typically to repay the SPV first
2. Any balance goes to **Public Trust**, which distributes it on order of the High Court (s98K(2))
3. After recovery, the undeveloped land is **excluded from the levy area** and the maximum levy revenue is reduced by the amount of IFF funding recovered (s98N)
4. The SPV must reduce either the levy period or the amount of levy in later years (s98N(2))

---

## 7. Who Benefits, Who Loses

| Party | Outcome |
|-------|---------|
| **SPV / bondholders** | Recover their funding rapidly. They designed the failure triggers in the levy order. They self-certify failure. They need only a $1,078 fee and a CEO-signed certificate. The sale is valid even if irregular. **They are the clear winners.** |
| **Developer/landowner** | Loses the land. Has only 20 working days to find what could be tens of millions of dollars. No judge reviews whether "failure" was fairly determined. If sold at auction at a distressed price (as forced sales typically are), they lose value beyond the SPV's claim. |
| **Mortgagee banks** | Their security (the land) is sold from under them. The proceeds go first to the SPV as specified in the levy order. The bank's mortgage may rank behind the SPV's claim, potentially leaving the bank with a shortfall. |
| **Other creditors** | Similar position to mortgagee banks -- subordinated to the SPV's statutory recovery right. |
| **Surrounding property owners** | Ambiguous. If the development failure is resolved through sale, a new developer might complete it. But a forced fire-sale may attract a buyer with no obligation to continue the original development, leaving surrounding owners with incomplete infrastructure and ongoing levy obligations. |
| **Future buyers of the land** | Buy at auction with no obligation to develop, and no obligation to check if the sale was proper (s98J(2)). Could be speculators or the SPV's own affiliates. |
| **Remaining levypayers** | After recovery, the levy area shrinks (s98N) and maximum revenue is reduced. But the infrastructure may already be partially built. If the recovery doesn't cover the SPV's costs, remaining levypayers may bear higher effective per-property costs for incomplete infrastructure. |
| **Public Trust** | Holds any surplus. But in a distressed forced sale, there is unlikely to be a surplus. |

## 8. What's Missing: Judicial Oversight

In a normal mortgagee sale or receivership in New Zealand, there are significant legal protections:

- A **judge** reviews the application
- The mortgagor has rights to apply to court to stop the sale
- The Property Law Act 2007 imposes duties of good faith and reasonable price
- Timeframes are typically months, not weeks
- The mortgagee owes a duty to obtain the best price reasonably obtainable

Under the Bill's accelerated recovery:

- The application goes to a **Registrar** (a court administrator), not a judge
- There is **no provision for the landowner to challenge** the SPV's determination that the development "failed"
- There is **no duty on the Registrar or SPV to obtain the best price** -- only that the bid meets the minimum repayment amount
- The sale is **valid even if irregular** (s98J) -- an extraordinary ouster of the usual judicial review grounds
- The only "judicial" involvement is if there's a surplus to distribute via Public Trust
- The landowner's **only practical recourse** would be judicial review of the SPV's original determination -- but by the time that case is heard, the land may already have been sold to a purchaser who has no obligation to inquire whether the sale was proper

### Comparison with existing NZ recovery processes

| Feature | Normal Mortgagee Sale | Rating Sale (existing) | IFF Accelerated Recovery |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|-------------------------|
| Decision maker | High Court judge | District Court | High Court **Registrar** |
| Notice period | 20+ working days + court time | Extensive (months) | 20 + 10 working days |
| Landowner can challenge | Yes, apply to court | Yes | **No explicit provision** |
| Duty to obtain best price | Yes (Property Law Act) | Yes | **No** -- minimum is repayment amount |
| Sale valid if irregular | No -- can be set aside | No -- can be set aside | **Yes** (s98J) |
| Independent assessment | Judge reviews merits | Court reviews | **SPV self-certifies** |

## 9. Concrete Scenario: LNG Terminal

Imagine the LNG terminal consortium's SPV has provided $500M in IFF funding. The levy order defines failure as "failure to commence terminal construction within 3 years." The project stalls due to resource consent delays or global LNG price collapse.

1. The SPV's own CEO signs a certificate saying the project has failed
2. The landowner (terminal developer) gets 20 working days to repay $500M + financing costs
3. No one can -- the whole reason the project failed is that funding dried up
4. The Registrar auctions the port-adjacent land at distressed prices
5. The SPV recovers what it can; any shortfall may fall on remaining levypayers
6. The landowner loses potentially valuable strategic land for a fraction of its long-term worth
7. The buyer at auction could be an affiliate of the original SPV investors, acquiring the land cheaply with no obligation to build the terminal
8. The sale is valid even if irregular -- the landowner has no practical remedy

### The circular risk

The mechanism creates a perverse incentive structure:

- The SPV **designs** the failure triggers at the outset (in the levy order)
- The SPV **determines** whether those triggers are met
- The SPV **self-certifies** the determination to the Registrar
- The SPV **benefits** from the forced sale (recovers its funding)
- The SPV can **consent** to a private treaty sale if the auction fails (s98H)

There is no independent party at any stage assessing whether the "failure" determination is fair, whether the landowner was given reasonable opportunity to cure, or whether the sale price reflects true value.

---

## 10. Broader Implications

The accelerated recovery mechanism functions, in effect, as a **private enforcement power with quasi-judicial authority but without judicial safeguards**. It grants a corporate entity (the SPV) powers that normally require judicial oversight:

1. The power to **determine default** (normally a court function)
2. The power to **compel repayment** on an extremely short timeline
3. The power to **force sale of real property** through an administrative process
4. The power to **validate irregular sales** by statutory fiat
5. The power to **consent to private treaty sales** when public processes fail

This is a remarkable grant of authority. While the stated purpose -- protecting SPV investors when developments fail -- is legitimate, the mechanism provides no balancing protections for landowners, creditors, or the public interest. The only constraint is the monitor receiving a copy of the notice (s98C(5)), but the monitor has no explicit power to intervene.
